# The House of Hulda Café: Himachali Home Cooking and Communal Breakfasts in the Orchard

There's no menu at House of Hulda's café. No laminated pages listing "Paneer Makhani" next to "Chow Mein." Instead, you come down to breakfast and there's warm bread from the tandoor, honey from somewhere local, cheese that tastes like milk and nothing else, fruit that's in season, and tea. The cook asks if you have allergies or preferences. Otherwise, she decides what you eat based on what's good today.

This is **Himachali home cooking** — the kind that feeds families and has for centuries, served at a café in Naggar where the rhythm of eating matches the rhythm of the mountains. No rush. No Instagram moments. Just food that tastes the way food is supposed to taste.

## What Is Himachali Cuisine

Himachali food is built on what the mountains provide. Rice is a staple (grown in the valleys below). Beans and lentils are preserved for winter. Local herbs — bathua, fenugreek, spinach — grow wild. Milk and cheese come from village herds. Honey comes from the bees. Chili is fresh or dried. Salt is salt.

The cooking is slow. Spice is measured. A dal simmers for hours, developing flavor from time rather than from garnish. A curry of potatoes or seasonal vegetables cooks until the spices have soaked into the vegetables, not just coated them. A rotli is rolled by hand and cooked on cast iron, puffing with air if you're skilled, which the cooks at House of Hulda are.

The result tastes like what it is: food from a place, made by hands that have been making it for decades.

Himachali cuisine also varies by region. The recipes in Naggar are specific to this village and the orchards around it. Food in a neighboring valley will have variations. This hyper-locality — the opposite of the homogenized "Indian restaurant" food you find in cities — is part of what makes eating here feel real.

## The Attic Café Philosophy

The attic café at House of Hulda has high ceilings (under the Kathkuni stone roof), large windows, and lanterns. The seating is ground-level cushions — the way people traditionally ate in Himalayan homes. There's no rush. Breakfast is 7–9 AM, not a time window but a rhythm. Lunch is whenever people are hungry and back from walking. Dinner is after dark, when the kitchen is most active.

The cook sources from:
- The orchard: apples, apricots (seasonal), sometimes berries
- Local farms: potatoes, carrots, seasonal vegetables
- Village vendors: dal, rice, flour
- Small producers: honey, cheese, sometimes yogurt

Nothing comes from a supplier 500 kilometers away. The food is what's growing now, what was stored from harvest, what local people eat.

The café also serves day visitors: you don't have to be a guest to eat breakfast or lunch here. People from Naggar village sometimes come for tea. Remote workers use it as an office. Travelers passing through pause for a meal. This is how it works best — guests and locals sharing tables, conversations, the ease of a place that's genuinely communal.

## What You Actually Eat at Breakfast

The menu changes daily, but the pattern is consistent:

**Bread**: Always fresh, made in the tandoor or on a griddle. This could be:
- Rotli (unleavened flat bread made with whole wheat)
- Roti with herbs mixed in (bathua rotli, if the herb is in season)
- Bread with ghee brushed on it
- Occasionally, a thinner bread, almost like a crepe, made with rice flour

The bread is warm, slightly smoky if it's from the tandoor, and generously buttered or served with ghee.

**Cheese and yogurt**: Usually fresh, from local sources. The cheese tastes like milk — clean, simple, sometimes slightly tangy. The yogurt is thick (not like the runny stuff in stores) and sometimes sweetened lightly with honey.

**Honey**: Local honey from the region, served in a small bowl. It's darker and more complex than commercial honey, with floral notes from mountain flowers.

**Fruit**: Whatever's in season. In spring, apples and apricots. In summer, sometimes berries or stone fruit. In autumn, apples from the orchard. In winter, stored apples or dried fruit soaked to plump.

**Vegetables**: Sometimes a light curry of seasonal vegetables — potatoes with greens, a simple eggplant preparation, spinach cooked with a little garlic and chili. Nothing heavy, just enough to feel substantial.

**Tea or coffee**: Real tea, brewed properly with milk and spice (cardamom, sometimes ginger). Or proper coffee, if you ask. Fresh lemon water if you prefer.

The whole meal is meant to fill you through the morning work or walking, without making you sluggish. It's breakfast designed for people who live in mountains, not for people rushing to offices.

## Lunch and Dinner

Lunch is lighter: often leftover bread, some dal, perhaps a vegetable curry, fresh fruit. You eat, you rest, the day continues.

Dinner is more elaborate: rice or rotli, at least one dal (beans, lentils, chickpeas), a vegetable curry, sometimes a second dish (often a simpler preparation — just potatoes roasted with salt and chili, or greens wilted with garlic). Yogurt. Achaar (pickle) — these are preserved vegetables that have fermented for months, providing digestive enzymes and sharp flavor.

The specific dishes rotate. Over a week at House of Hulda, you might eat:
- Rice and moong dal (with turmeric and cumin)
- Roti and rajma (kidney beans, slow-cooked with tomato and spice)
- Rotli and a vegetable curry (seasonal), perhaps with potatoes and spinach
- Rice and arhar dal (pigeon peas), often made with a touch of jaggery for subtle sweetness
- A chickpea curry (chana masala-adjacent but lighter, the Himachali version)
- Local greens (bathua or fenugreek) cooked simply

The food is vegetarian. House of Hulda serves vegetarian meals by default (this reflects local practice; the region has vegetarian traditions). If you eat meat and want to arrange it, discuss with the host in advance.

Breakfast and dinner are communal. You sit at long tables with other guests. Conversations happen. Travelers exchange routes and recommendations. A writer talks to a remote worker. A local person shares a story with visitors.

This isn't forced. You're not assigned seats or required to speak. Some mornings are quiet — people eat and read. Some mornings the table erupts into conversation. The structure allows for both.

Over multiple visits or longer stays, the communal aspect deepens. People cook together. Someone asks to help make bread. The kitchen opens up. The boundary between "restaurant" and "home" dissolves.

If you prefer privacy, you can take food to your room (it works, but the café is warmer and lighter). The default, though, is to eat together — not as a requirement but as an option that's available.

## The Taste of Himachali Food (and Why It Matters)

Industrial food is designed to be consistent. The tomato tastes the same whether you eat it in winter or summer; the dal is the same whether it's May or December. This consistency requires enormous infrastructure: refrigeration, preservation, transportation, chemicals.

Himachali home cooking is the opposite. The food tastes different in September (new harvest) than in March (stored food). An apple in October tastes like it was just picked. A dal in February tastes like it's been stored and reconstituted, which it has — but that's the reality of mountain living, and the food reflects it.

This variability is often described as "limitation," but it's actually information. Your body knows it's eating what the place is producing right now. You know you're eating in season. Nutritionally, you're getting what your body needs at that time of year (new harvest provides certain nutrients, stored foods provide others). It's not a coincidence that food traditions evolved to match the seasons; they're optimal.

The taste is also a function of method. Food cooked slowly, in cast iron, with ghee or oil, over wood or coals, develops flavors that quick cooking doesn't. A dal simmered for three hours tastes different (richer, deeper) than a dal made in a pressure cooker in 20 minutes. Both are dal. One tastes like it's been loved.

## Learning to Cook Himachali Food

If you're a guest and curious about cooking, you can ask to learn. This isn't a formal "cooking class"; it's an invitation to stand in the kitchen and watch someone who knows the food make it. You ask questions. You help if you want. You learn by doing, not by being lectured.

What you might learn:
- How to make rotli by hand (it's harder than it looks, but possible)
- The order in which to add spices to a dal (timing matters for flavor)
- How to tell when a vegetable curry is done (taste, not time)
- The difference between a vegetable cooked until soft and a vegetable cooked until it falls apart (it's a choice)
- How to store and use preserved foods (chutney, achaar, dried herbs)
- What herbs are edible and growing wild around the village

Most importantly: cooking is slow, intentional, and connected to place. You're not optimizing for time; you're optimizing for taste and for the pleasure of eating together.

## The Ritual Around Food

At House of Hulda, eating has rhythm:

**Breakfast (early morning)**: Energy and light and the day still young. Conversation if you want, silence if you don't.

**Lunch (midday)**: Quieter, often eaten by individuals or small groups. The time for resting after work or walking.

**Evening tea (around 4 PM)**: A light tea, sometimes with a snack. A pause before dinner.

**Dinner (after sunset)**: The full meal. The whole house gathers. Stories are told. The day is reviewed. It's the main communal event.

This rhythm isn't modern. It's ancient in the mountains. Work in the morning and afternoon. Rest when the light dims. Gather when it's dark. Eat the largest meal when you can sit together.

Living according to this rhythm — even for a week — recalibrates something in your body. You realize how much of normal life is eating on the run, eating alone, eating while doing something else. This is different. Eating is the thing you're doing. Everything else pauses.

## Beyond the Café: Food Culture in Naggar

If you stay in Naggar, you'll notice the village itself has deep food traditions. Locals grow apples, make cheese, preserve vegetables. Markets have seasonal patterns — certain vegetables appear in spring, disappear by summer. Food isn't separated from living; it's integrated into it.

Small vendors sell fresh vegetables from gardens. The dhaba (roadside café) serves local fare to workers. Homes have orchards and kitchen gardens. Food is personal and local.

Eating at House of Hulda's café is a window into this — not tourist food pretending to be local, but actual local food, eaten by locals, in the way they eat it.

## What You Learn by Eating This Way

After eating Himachali home cooking for a week or more, many visitors report:

- **Less sugar craving**: Simple food satisfies more. You don't need dessert after a meal of dal and vegetables and bread.
- **Better digestion**: The spices (especially cumin, asafetida, ginger) aid digestion. You feel lighter.
- **Deeper sleep**: The food is nutritious without being heavy. You sleep better.
- **Clearer thinking**: No post-meal energy crash because there's no refined sugar or excess fat.
- **Connection to place**: You eat apples from the orchard. You eat dal made from beans grown nearby. You know where the food comes from.
- **Slower eating**: Without devices at the table, you chew more. You taste more. Eating takes 30 minutes instead of 10.

These aren't health claims; they're observations. The food works because it evolved in this place, for people living in this place. You're temporarily living in this place, so the food works for you too.
