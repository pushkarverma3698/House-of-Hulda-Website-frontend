Frontend audit / House of Hulda / Scroll film pipeline / 17 Aug 2026

The design is fine. The film is 720 px wide.

The blur is not a grading choice, a codec setting, or a scroll-smoothing artifact. The hero film is a 9:16 mobile master being stretched across landscape desktop screens — and for most of the scroll the browser is showing you a 160 × 284 thumbnail of it.

Verdict

Nothing is wrong with the art direction. On a phone this site looks exactly like the thing you designed: sharp, full-frame, premium. On a desktop it falls apart — and it falls apart for reasons that are geometric, not aesthetic. No amount of shader, grade, or easing work will fix it, because the pixels being asked for do not exist in the source.

How I tested

Production build, Chromium, three viewports: desktop 1920×1080 at DPR 1 and DPR 2, and iPhone-class 390×844 at DPR 1.5. Two scroll modes at each — a slow deliberate read with a full stop at fifteen positions, and a real momentum flick. Sharpness is measured as Laplacian variance on identical 600 px crops of the same source frame, so the numbers compare like with like.

F-01 Blocker

A portrait film on a landscape screen

Every frame in public/frames/hero/ is 720×1280 — vertical, 9:16, shot for a phone. The canvas draws it with cover-fit. On a 16:9 desktop that does two things at once, and both of them hurt.

The second cost is resolution. To cover 1920 px of width from a 720 px source the browser scales up 2.67×, and on a Retina/4K display where the backing store is 3840 px wide, 5.33×. That is the best case — the sharpest tier the pipeline has, on a completely stationary page.

F-02 Blocker

While you scroll, you are watching a thumbnail

The scrubber runs a three-tier ladder — hero-proxy at 160×284, hero-mid at 320×568, hero at 720×1280 — and picks a tier by scroll velocity. The proxy tier exists so the cache never misses during a fast flick. It works: playback is genuinely smooth, zero dropped frames, zero evictions.

But smooth is not the problem. On desktop, that 160 px-wide proxy is being blown up 24× to fill a Retina backing store. That is what the page looks like during every single scroll gesture.

F-03 Blocker

On desktop, the sharp frame almost never arrives

The design intent is that stopping the scroll swaps in the 720 px frame after 140 ms — the image "focuses". I scrolled to ten positions and waited 2.5 seconds at each, roughly eighteen times the settle threshold.

Instrumenting the network layer showed why: on desktop, after jumping to a new position and sitting perfectly still for six seconds, the sharp frame for that position was never even requested. Mobile requested it and had it decoded in 8 ms.

The render loop is not broken — it is starved. Disabling the WebGL layer restores it completely:

The page stacks two full-screen canvases: a 2D canvas doing a fullscreen blit every frame, and on top of it a WebGL canvas running bloom, noise, chromatic aberration and vignette across the whole viewport, every frame, forever. The second one is starving the first — and what it buys visually is stars, embers, and faint fog.

One caveat, stated plainly. This container renders WebGL in software (SwiftShader), which exaggerates GPU cost. The direction of the effect is real and the isolation is clean, but the exact frame rates will be better on your machine. Worth re-running on real hardware before sizing the fix — the architectural point stands either way.
F-04 Blocker

Every visitor outside India gets a night sky over afternoon footage

Those coloured specks scattered across the daylight drone shot in the desktop captures above are not JPEG artifacts. They are the star field, at full opacity, at 15:40 in the afternoon.

getDateAtT() builds the scene time with new Date(y, m, d, 15, 40) — a local timestamp in the viewer's timezone — then asks SunCalc for the sun's position over Naggar at that instant. The sun's altitude therefore depends on where the visitor is sitting, not where the house is.

For a visitor in London or New York, the entire day-to-night arc — the single strongest idea in the whole build — plays backwards or not at all. The HUD says 15:40 while the readout beside it says SUN ALT −24°, the star field is at full brightness, and the night grade is fully applied over a sunlit valley.

For a premium homestay whose booking audience is substantially international, this is the version of the site most prospective guests see.

The fix is small and self-contained: compute the scene clock at a fixed +05:30 offset instead of the viewer's local offset, so the film's timeline is anchored to Naggar the way the copy already claims it is.

F-05 Major

The last third of the scroll is a single frozen frame

The film maps to t < 0.7. From t = 0.7 onward the canvas holds frame 240 and then fades it out. On a 950 svh page that is roughly 2,750 px — about two and a half full screens — of scrolling against one static, upscaled image.

It lands on exactly the wrong three sections: The Eighteen Gods, The Valley Commons, and the closing Reserve the Stay call to action. The stargazing centrepiece — telescope telemetry, Bortle Class 1, eighteen deities — is presented over a frozen, blurry, brightly-lit bedroom interior with no night sky behind it at all.

The film runs out precisely where the site starts asking for the booking.

F-06 Minor

65 MB of unused 4K landscape frames are shipping in the repo

public/frames/phase1 (3840×2160), phase2 and phase3 (2560×1440) hold 300 landscape frames that no component references. They are real handheld footage — hazy midday, a different look entirely — so they are not a drop-in replacement for the drone film. But they are dead weight in the deploy, and they confirm the pipeline can already produce landscape 4K frames.

Next

What actually fixes this

F-01 is an asset problem, not a code problem. This is the part worth being blunt about: no shader, no sharpening pass, no cache tuning, no easing curve will recover detail that was never captured. Everything else on this page is worth doing, and none of it will make the film look sharp on a desktop until there is a landscape master to draw from.

Recommended — re-master the film in landscape

Re-render or re-generate the hero sequence at 16:9, 2560 px wide or better, and keep the existing 9:16 master as the mobile art direction. Serve the two by media query — the tier ladder already handles everything else. This restores both the sharpness and the composition, including the snowline that desktop visitors have never seen. It is the only change that addresses F-01 at all.

In parallel — cheap, high-yield

  • Anchor the scene clock to IST (F-04). Small, isolated, and it repairs the day-to-night arc for every international visitor.
  • Cut the WebGL post-processing on desktop, or drop it to DPR 1 (F-03). Bloom, noise and chromatic aberration over an already-graded film are buying very little and costing the sharp tier.
  • Raise the proxy tier. 160 px was sized for a 585 px phone backing store. Desktop needs its own rung — the memory budget maths in ScrollCanvas assumes mobile constraints throughout.
  • Extend or re-time the film past t = 0.7 (F-05), so the closing sections have live footage behind them.
  • Drop phase1–3 from the deploy (F-06) — 65 MB for nothing.

Stopgap, if a re-master is weeks away

Stop stretching the portrait film across the full desktop viewport. Present it as a centred 9:16 panel — full frame, near 1:1, letterboxed into a graded field — and let the composition survive at the cost of scale. It is a real design change and needs your sign-off, but it looks deliberate, where the current full-bleed stretch reads as broken.

The encouraging part. The engineering underneath is good. Zero dropped frames, zero cache evictions, 100% frame delivery, a genuinely well-reasoned memory ladder with the arithmetic written into the comments. It was all tuned against a phone, and it does that job well. The desktop case was simply never in the frame.
